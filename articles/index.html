Agentic commerceOctober 31, 2026

The Future of E-Commerce: Your Next Customer May Never Browse Your Website

For roughly seven months, I used my personal OpenClaw bot, to help with shopping research.

OpenClaw gave me my first experience of delegating product discovery and comparison to an AI agent. I learned what was useful, what tended to go wrong, and when I still needed to check a retailer’s website myself.

I then started using Meta Muse

After 38 days, I asked Muse to summarize my shopping activity. It reported:

  • 47 product searches
  • 1,252 listings examined
  • 220 retailer websites explored
  • 248 listings from Home Depot
  • 115 listings from Walmart

The most interesting result wasn’t which retailer had the lowest price or largest offering. It was which retailers the agent could actually work with.

If the agent couldn’t verify a product’s specifications, availability, total price, delivery date, or return policy, that product usually disappeared from my shortlist.

This wasn’t a scientific retailer ranking. The results were influenced by what I was shopping for, my location and preferences, and which websites Muse could access. The numbers represent listings researched, not purchases.

Still, that access limitation turned out to be the most revealing part of the experiment.

The Agent Decided What I Saw but gave me exactly what I Need to see

Retail websites are designed for people.They have navigation menus, search boxes, filters, recommendations, product pages, and carefully designed checkout flows. Retailers have spent years improving those experiences.

But when I delegated the initial research to RaOne and later to Muse, my experience with a retailer began before I ever saw its website.

For many searches, I never opened the retailer’s site at all. I saw the products the agent could find and verify.

That changed what mattered.

A beautiful product page wasn’t very useful if the agent couldn’t confirm the model number, dimensions, compatibility, local inventory, delivery date, or total price.

The human experience still matters. I want to recognize the retailer, trust the store, and understand the offer before I buy.

But the agent may decide which retailers I consider in the first place.

Why Home Depot and Walmart Kept Appearing

Home Depot and Walmart appeared more often than most other retailers in my Muse results.

Some of that was probably caused by my shopping mix, location, search results, and the size of their catalogs. The numbers don’t prove that they are the most agent-friendly retailers.

But Muse was often able to retrieve useful product specifications, pricing, local availability, and pickup or delivery options from them.

On some other retail sites, access restrictions or verification steps prevented the same level of research.

When that happened, the product often fell out of the comparison.

The retailer may have had a better product or a lower price. I simply never got far enough to know.

Other retailers Risk Is Losing the Starting Point

Large retailer built its position by bringing a huge number of sellers into one searchable marketplace.

Shopping agents could change what “the marketplace” means.

An agent doesn’t have to begin and end inside one retailer. It can compare Amazon with Walmart, Home Depot, Best Buy, specialty retailers, manufacturers, local stores, and other reputable sellers across the internet.

In other words, the whole web starts to look like one marketplace.

The risk is that Amazon may no longer automatically own the beginning of the product search.It becomes one very strong option in a much larger comparison.

If another reputable retailer has the right product, a lower total price, faster local pickup, or a better return policy, the agent can recommend it without requiring the customer to search elsewhere.

What Makes a Retailer Useful to an Agent?

Looking back at my searches, most of the good and bad experiences came down to six questions:

  1. Can the agent find and access the product?
  2. Are the specifications, model numbers, and variants clear?
  3. Is the product actually available online or nearby?
  4. What is the real price after shipping, fees, and promotion conditions?
  5. When can the customer receive or pick it up?
  6. What happens if the product is wrong?

The final question mattered more than I expected.

The cheapest product wasn’t always the best recommendation. I would often rather pay slightly more for same-day pickup and a simple return than save a few dollars on an item with uncertain delivery and a vague return policy.

Agents aren’t simply comparing products. They’re comparing the likely outcome of the entire transaction.

From SEO to Agent Readiness

Retailers already optimize for Google, advertising, and website conversion.

They may now need to consider how their stores appear to shopping agents.

Call it agent optimization, agent readiness, or something else. The name matters less than the work involved:

  • Complete and structured product information
  • Consistent model and variant names
  • Accurate inventory
  • Transparent total pricing
  • Reliable delivery estimates
  • Clear return policies
  • Secure ways for authorized agents to complete transactions

Being mentioned in an AI-generated answer isn’t enough. The underlying information must be dependable enough for an agent to recommend the product.

Retailers now have two experiences to design. The human experience must create understanding and trust. The agent experience must provide information that is accessible, complete, current, and verifiable.

I started using these agents because I wanted a better way to shop.

I came away with a different question:

How many retailers are ready for customers whose first—and perhaps most important—interaction with their store happens through an AI agent?

Generative UIJune 20, 2026

Adaptive Storefront - Continued

After the first post - Spent a little bit more time on Adaptive Storefront.

The original version showed the same home-improvement storefront changing for different shoppers: appliance buying, gift shopping, and repair support. The point was that those pages were not hardcoded as separate templates.

There is no fixed "dishwasher page" or "gift page" sitting behind the UI. The app exposes a small set of rendering tools and the AI path can compose the page through those tools at runtime.

The part I wanted to push further was this: can the app adapt while the session is changing, not only after someone lands with a established persona?

That matters for conversion from browse to Orders . A lot of shoppers are not lost because there wan't the product they needed . They are lost because the page makes them do too much translation. They know they have a leaking faucet or need a dishwasher or need a useful gift and we still don't surface things they care about.

If the page can answer some of those questions before the customer has to ask, that is real leverage. For known intents which is majority of us shoppers we know what we are looking for already and are focussed on it - No chat required. No long search journey. No "ask me more about this" wizard , if we can highlight even 60% of the info they require without asking

The analogy I keep thinking about is an airport. If you land in a new airport, the best experience is not always finding a person and explaining where you are trying to go. Sometimes the best experience is better signage: clear gates, transfer paths, baggage directions, and the right cues appearing at the right moment. You can still ask someone for help but you should not need to for the common journeys.

That is how I think about this storefront. Chat can still exist, but the application itself should carry more of the guidance.

The new version adds live intent drift. The app watches lightweight session signals like search input, search submit, category clicks, and product page views. Those feed a simple rule-based scorer for intents such as repair, gift, outdoor, project, and starter.

When an intent starts to look likely, the app can generate that page in the background. If the signal gets stronger, it can switch to that experience. If the page was already built, the switch feels instant.

This is still a GenUI experiment, but not in the "model writes React" sense. Each tool maps to a section the app already knows how to render. The model can decide that an appliance buyer needs comparison first, then install accessories, then a buying checklist. But it cannot invent a fake component or a fake product.

That boundary is the whole design. The model plans. React renders. The app validates. The catalog grounds the product truth.

I also think this is a better framing than plain recommendation. Recommendation usually asks, "Which products should rank higher?" That is useful, but limited. This demo asks, "What should this page become so the customer can make progress faster?"

For a repair shopper, the answer may be a guide-first page. For a gift shopper, curation may matter more. For an appliance buyer, comparison may need to come before browsing.

The product list changes, but so does the decision support around it.

I kept the system deliberately less magical than it could sound. Product IDs are checked against a real 40-product catalog. Tool arguments are validated with Zod schemas. If generation fails or the API key is missing, the app falls back to deterministic layouts.

That is the version of AI in product surfaces I am most interested in: not unlimited generation, but bounded orchestration. Another thing I added is more evidence in the UI. Adaptive pages can get weird quickly if no one understands why they changed. The demo now has a signal evidence bar, an "Optimized for..." intent banner, an intent trail, a debug panel for live scores, and the persona picker for explicit testing.

That is partly for the demo, but it is also a product lesson. If an experience adapts, the user and the team need some way to inspect the reasoning.

The interesting version of GenUI is not "let the model do anything." It is: let the model make bounded experience decisions inside constraints the product already owns.

The first post showed the screenshots. This update adds more of the system behavior around them: live intent tracking, background generation, cached page states, intent switching, and better inspectability.

The question I keep coming back to is:

Where can the product reduce customer effort by becoming more contextual, without giving up control of the experience?

AI operating modelsMay 23, 2026

Starbucks AI Inventory Story Was Really a Supply Chain Reliability Lesson

Why the Starbucks AI inventory story is not really an AI failure story.

Starbucks recently retired its AI-powered inventory counting tool across North America after a nine-month rollout.

Most headlines focused on the visible failure mode: the system reportedly miscounted or mislabeled items, confused similar milk types and even missed a peppermint syrup bottle while scanning nearby bottles.

But the deeper issue is upstream.

From what I took away from the Reuters report , Starbucks has been dealing with supply-chain reliability problem: deliveries to distribution centers were reportedly arriving on time and complete far below what a healthy operating network would expect. 33% accuracy - 1 in 3 trucks were on time with full order to their Distribution Center.

That changes how I read the AI story.

If the replenishment system itself is unreliable, perfect shelf-level visibility does not solve the operating problem. You can know with absolute precision that oat milk is empty and still be unable to replenish it reliably.

Software instruments a system. It does not automatically fix the system.

The architectural pattern is familiar:

Sensor layer: The AI has to classify fine-grained products from packaging that may not be standardized or designed to be machine-readable.

Integration layer: The output has to flow into legacy inventory and ordering systems quickly enough to close the loop.

Actuator layer: The supply chain has to actually execute the replenishment signal.

In this case, AI was deployed at the sensor layer while the integration and actuator layers still had structural weaknesses.

That is where many enterprise AI deployments struggle.

The model becomes the most visible part of the system, so it gets blamed first. But the model’s limit is often set by the least reliable part of the operational workflow around it.

Over the last few years, Starbucks reportedly tried multiple inventory and ordering initiatives:

  1. ML-based demand forecasting and automated ordering - o9
  2. A refinement effort called “Never-Out”
  3. AI-powered automated inventory counting - NomadGo

Different tools. Same operating problem.

That is the pattern worth paying attention to. When multiple technology layers are introduced into the same workflow and each one struggles to scale, the issue is usually not just the latest tool.

It is the system around the tool.

This is the lesson I take from it as an engineer:

Before deploying AI into an operational process, map the full operating loop.

AI does not create value just because it makes a prediction.

It creates value when that prediction changes an outcome.

Ask:

  1. What is the AI detecting ?
  2. Where does that signal go and who consumes it for what ?
  3. What action should happen next ?
  4. How often does that action actually complete correctly?

That last question is usually the most important one.

If the model detects the issue correctly, but the downstream system cannot act reliably, the AI will expose the problem faster than it solves it.

This is also why I’m focused in my own work on improving on-time and complete delivery outcomes. AI can make a working system better, but the foundation still matters.

I build with AI every day at work and on side projects, and I’m currently scoping a synthetic data project for fine-grained product classification — the same type of problem this inventory tool faced for not recognizing peppermint syrup bottle while scanning nearby bottles but that all be for another day.

Even if the AI correctly identifies the item — for example, “this store is out of oat milk” — the real question is whether the rest of the business system can do something useful with that information

Instrument AI to learn. Fix the system. Then scale the AI

AI-assisted engineeringMay 16, 2026

One Refactor, Two AI Assistants 5000+ lines

It's Saturday - A new AI coding assistant was introduced at work last week.
So naturally, I did what any curious engineer would do:
I turned the 5,552 line Python file I had been avoiding into an experiment.

I had a very normal engineering question:

If two AI coding assistants get the same repo, the same prompt, the same task, and the same tests...

I tested this on a Python file that had grown to 5,552 lines.

Before anyone judges the number: this was not a “I woke up and chose chaos” situation. It was the classic growth story. A working Streamlit view- few more workflows , few more UI states , few more helpers. Tests added along the way. Features kept shipping. Eventually the file still worked. The tests still passed.The product still did what it needed to do.

Lines of code are a terrible quality metric but a useful smoke alarm.They tell you where to look at atleast. To be clear, I was not trying to make total LOC go down. Splitting a file often adds imports, wrappers and compatibility code. The point was manageable load not a line-count trophy. LOC was just the smoke alarm that told me where to inspect.

But opening the file started to feel like walking into Costco without aisle signs.
Everything I needed was probably in there.
Somewhere between snacks, clothes and a giant hanging dragon swimming slide.

So I turned it into a small experiment. Same starting point. Same prompt.Two separate worktrees.

Two enterprise-approved coding assistants

One task:

“Split this monolith into smaller modules, preserve behavior, and keep the tests green.” I also used sub-agents as part of the workflow, not to blindly generate more code, but to pressure-test the plan preserving context:

  • one looked at the best module boundaries
  • one looked for import and test risks
  • one helped compare the final results against the actual diff

That part matters. A good AI workflow is not just “ask model, accept answer.” It is designing the review loop around the model.

Both assistants understood the assignment. Both produced a similar package layout:

  • phase modules
  • UI components
  • dialogs
  • a compatibility layer
  • preserved import paths
  • passing tests

So at first glance, it looked like a tie.

Then I did what any responsible engineer would do: I asked the assistant to run the highly sophisticated enterprise-grade evaluation tool known as:
`wc -l`

Assistant A produced a split package around 6,939 lines.

Assistant B produced one around 9,600 lines.

Same general shape. Same goal. Very different luggage policy.

Assistant A packed like a solo hiker charger, hoodie, protein bar done.

Assistant B packed like a parent with two kids and kept everything they might need irrespective of needing or not - change of clothes , snacks for 2 days, rain jackets with no rain till next day , their fav toy etc

The funny part is that Assistant B was not useless. It was cautious. It preserved behavior. It had a working structure. It just carried a lot more scaffolding than necessary, including large repeated import blocks.

Now, could I have asked Assistant A to verify its own work and improve it further? Yes.

Could I have asked Assistant B to clean up the extra imports and shrink the diff? Also yes.

But that was not the objective of this experiment.

The objective was to see what each assistant produced from the same prompt, under the same conditions, without giving one of them extra correction rounds.

That is the only way the comparison stays fair.

If one assistant gets a second prompt, a cleanup prompt, a lint prompt, and a “please think harder” prompt, while the other gets one shot, I am not comparing tools anymore.

I am comparing my patience. That was the real learning. “Tests pass” is necessary, but it is not the finish line.

The sub-agent reviews helped make the comparison sharper.

That is the part I want to get better at as an AI engineer. Not just prompting and accepting , trying to find what agents usually get wrong and what i should watch for

A few takeaways I will reuse:

  1. Run AI tools side by side when the task matters. The comparison is often more valuable than either single answer.
  1. Passing tests prove behavior, not maintainability. You still need lint, diff review and HUMAN judgment.
  1. Sub-agents are useful when they have specific jobs. “Review the import risk” is better than “look at this.”
  1. AI assistants have personalities - Some are minimal. Some are cautious. Some bring a folding chair to a standing meeting.

If I had only used one assistant, I probably would have have asked it to review and if the review didn't catch i probably would have missed in my manual PR as well trusting the changes.

Voice AI · RetailJuly 2026

A retail copilot that stays out of the conversation

I built a prototype retail associate copilot around one constraint: the AI should strengthen the human interaction without joining it. The system shadows the associate–customer conversation, then delivers a short private cue through an earpiece only when there is a real retail need.

The common path is deliberately unglamorous and fast. Silero VAD detects completed speech, transcription produces the query, and a local FTS5/BM25 catalog search with trigram typo recovery and structured filters resolves locations, prices, inventory, promotions, and store services. A grounded tool-using model is reserved for genuinely ambiguous requests. Greetings, small talk, and uncertain noise stay silent.

Conversation memory lets follow-ups such as “the two-inch one” retain context. Every turn records the chosen route and the latency spent in VAD, speech-to-text, retrieval, the model, and text-to-speech. The useful lesson was not that every interaction needs an LLM. It was that the product needs an observable way to decide when one is worth the cost and delay.

What I was testing: Can an assistant add useful context without competing with the associate for the customer’s attention?

Computer vision · Local AISummer 2026

MealWatch: local computer vision at the dinner table

After saying “eat your food” for what felt like the forty-seventh time, I turned dinner into a computer-vision experiment. MealWatch observes each child independently through a webcam and gives a gentle reminder when someone has stopped eating, become distracted, stood up, or left the frame.

YOLO pose estimation supplies the body landmarks. A hand-to-mouth state machine looks for bites, while a separate tracker maintains each child’s state: eating, idle, distracted, fidgeting, standing, or out of frame. Tiered reminders escalate only after continued inactivity. A FastAPI backend streams events to a Next.js dashboard over WebSockets, so the camera view, per-child state, and event history stay current.

The entire pipeline runs locally on macOS—no video is uploaded and no cloud service is required. The playful premise forced a serious systems problem: real-time inference is only useful when identity, temporal state, false positives, and intervention timing are handled together.

What I was testing: How do you turn noisy pose signals into restrained, stateful behavior for multiple people in real time?

Voice AI · GamesSummer 2026

Sparkle’s Magic Game Maker: letting kids invent the game

Watching my children play games during summer break led to a different question: why couldn’t they make the game themselves, using the characters and worlds already in their heads? I built Sparkle, an animated fairy who interviews children by voice and turns their answers into a playable browser game.

The experience narrows an open-ended idea into choices a young child can make: gameplay style, hero, world, collectibles, obstacles, and a title. The server uses those decisions to generate a complete HTML5 game, while the browser handles speech recognition and narration. Later experiments added browser-side keyword spotting with TensorFlow.js so voice controls could respond without a server round trip.

Four constrained gameplay patterns—runner, flyer, dodge, and defender—made the output more reliable than asking a model to invent any game from scratch. The hardest part was not generation. It was preserving a child’s sense of authorship while keeping the result understandable, playable, and fast enough to feel magical.

What I was testing: Can structured AI generation give children creative control without handing them a prompt box?

Accessibility engineeringJuly 2026

Making shared-screen color understandable without color

Picture a production incident: twenty people, one shared dashboard, and a tangle of colored lines. When someone says “look at the red line,” a colorblind participant can lose the thread of the conversation. I built a small macOS tool to make that moment easier.

The app captures one selected window and creates a live, always-on-top mirror at roughly twelve frames per second. Daltonization-based correction can be tuned for deuteranopia, protanopia, or tritanopia. Hovering over the mirror finds the nearest data color, shows its human-readable name and RGB value, and magnifies the sampled area. This adds a textual cue instead of assuming recoloring alone will always be enough.

macOS already provides system-wide color filters. This experiment explored the missing workflow: correct one shared window, compare it beside the original, and name the color explicitly. It is less an AI showcase than an example of the product instinct I want AI systems to retain—start with the person who is being excluded, then build the smallest useful bridge.

What I was testing: Can a local assistive layer make color-dependent collaboration understandable without changing everyone else’s screen?
