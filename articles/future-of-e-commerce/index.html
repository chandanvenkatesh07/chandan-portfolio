Agentic commerce

The Future of E-Commerce: Your Next Customer May Never Browse Your Website

Chandan VenkateshOctober 31, 2026

For roughly seven months, I used my personal OpenClaw bot, to help with shopping research.

OpenClaw gave me my first experience of delegating product discovery and comparison to an AI agent. I learned what was useful, what tended to go wrong, and when I still needed to check a retailer’s website myself.

I then started using Meta Muse

After 38 days, I asked Muse to summarize my shopping activity. It reported:

  • 47 product searches
  • 1,252 listings examined
  • 220 retailer websites explored
  • 248 listings from Home Depot
  • 115 listings from Walmart

The most interesting result wasn’t which retailer had the lowest price or largest offering. It was which retailers the agent could actually work with.

If the agent couldn’t verify a product’s specifications, availability, total price, delivery date, or return policy, that product usually disappeared from my shortlist.

This wasn’t a scientific retailer ranking. The results were influenced by what I was shopping for, my location and preferences, and which websites Muse could access. The numbers represent listings researched, not purchases.

Still, that access limitation turned out to be the most revealing part of the experiment.

The Agent Decided What I Saw but gave me exactly what I Need to see

Retail websites are designed for people.They have navigation menus, search boxes, filters, recommendations, product pages, and carefully designed checkout flows. Retailers have spent years improving those experiences.

But when I delegated the initial research to RaOne and later to Muse, my experience with a retailer began before I ever saw its website.

For many searches, I never opened the retailer’s site at all. I saw the products the agent could find and verify.

That changed what mattered.

A beautiful product page wasn’t very useful if the agent couldn’t confirm the model number, dimensions, compatibility, local inventory, delivery date, or total price.

The human experience still matters. I want to recognize the retailer, trust the store, and understand the offer before I buy.

But the agent may decide which retailers I consider in the first place.

Why Home Depot and Walmart Kept Appearing

Home Depot and Walmart appeared more often than most other retailers in my Muse results.

Some of that was probably caused by my shopping mix, location, search results, and the size of their catalogs. The numbers don’t prove that they are the most agent-friendly retailers.

But Muse was often able to retrieve useful product specifications, pricing, local availability, and pickup or delivery options from them.

On some other retail sites, access restrictions or verification steps prevented the same level of research.

When that happened, the product often fell out of the comparison.

The retailer may have had a better product or a lower price. I simply never got far enough to know.

Other retailers Risk Is Losing the Starting Point

Large retailer built its position by bringing a huge number of sellers into one searchable marketplace.

Shopping agents could change what “the marketplace” means.

An agent doesn’t have to begin and end inside one retailer. It can compare Amazon with Walmart, Home Depot, Best Buy, specialty retailers, manufacturers, local stores, and other reputable sellers across the internet.

In other words, the whole web starts to look like one marketplace.

The risk is that Amazon may no longer automatically own the beginning of the product search.It becomes one very strong option in a much larger comparison.

If another reputable retailer has the right product, a lower total price, faster local pickup, or a better return policy, the agent can recommend it without requiring the customer to search elsewhere.

What Makes a Retailer Useful to an Agent?

Looking back at my searches, most of the good and bad experiences came down to six questions:

  1. Can the agent find and access the product?
  2. Are the specifications, model numbers, and variants clear?
  3. Is the product actually available online or nearby?
  4. What is the real price after shipping, fees, and promotion conditions?
  5. When can the customer receive or pick it up?
  6. What happens if the product is wrong?

The final question mattered more than I expected.

The cheapest product wasn’t always the best recommendation. I would often rather pay slightly more for same-day pickup and a simple return than save a few dollars on an item with uncertain delivery and a vague return policy.

Agents aren’t simply comparing products. They’re comparing the likely outcome of the entire transaction.

From SEO to Agent Readiness

Retailers already optimize for Google, advertising, and website conversion.

They may now need to consider how their stores appear to shopping agents.

Call it agent optimization, agent readiness, or something else. The name matters less than the work involved:

  • Complete and structured product information
  • Consistent model and variant names
  • Accurate inventory
  • Transparent total pricing
  • Reliable delivery estimates
  • Clear return policies
  • Secure ways for authorized agents to complete transactions

Being mentioned in an AI-generated answer isn’t enough. The underlying information must be dependable enough for an agent to recommend the product.

Retailers now have two experiences to design. The human experience must create understanding and trust. The agent experience must provide information that is accessible, complete, current, and verifiable.

I started using these agents because I wanted a better way to shop.

I came away with a different question:

How many retailers are ready for customers whose first—and perhaps most important—interaction with their store happens through an AI agent?
